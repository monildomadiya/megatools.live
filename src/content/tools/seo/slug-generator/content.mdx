## What a slug is

The slug is the human-readable part of a URL that identifies one specific page:

```
https://example.com/tools/seo/slug-generator
                             └──────┬──────┘
                                  slug
```

It is the only part of a URL you fully control and the only part a reader might actually read. A good one describes the page in a few words, survives being copied into an email, and does not change.

## What is actually legal

RFC 3986, the specification that defines URI syntax, splits characters into groups. The **unreserved** set can appear anywhere without encoding:

```
unreserved = ALPHA / DIGIT / "-" / "." / "_" / "~"
```

Letters, digits, hyphen, period, underscore, tilde. That is the complete list.

Other characters fall into two camps. Some are **reserved** because they have structural meaning — `/` separates path segments, `?` starts the query string, `#` starts the fragment. Using them in a slug does not fail; it changes what the URL means.

Everything else must be **percent-encoded**: converted to its UTF-8 bytes and written as `%` followed by hex. A space becomes `%20`. An `é` becomes `%C3%A9`. An emoji becomes four percent-escapes.

Browsers hide this. They display the decoded form in the address bar, which is why a URL can look clean on screen and arrive as an unreadable string when pasted into a plain-text email.

The practical conclusion: restrict slugs to **lowercase letters, digits and hyphens**. That is a subset of the unreserved set, so nothing is ever encoded, nothing is ever ambiguous, and the URL looks the same everywhere.

## Hyphens, not underscores

Both are legal and unreserved. Google's URL structure documentation recommends hyphens, on the grounds that they help users and search engines identify the separate concepts in a URL, while underscores have a long history of *joining* words in programming identifiers — `format_date` is one name, not two words.

This is a convention rather than a technical requirement, but it is the convention search engines were built around, and there is no offsetting benefit to underscores. Use hyphens.

## Lowercase, always

This one prevents a real class of bug rather than being a style preference.

Domain names are case-insensitive. **Paths are not** — on most servers, `/About-Us` and `/about-us` are two different URLs.

If both resolve, you have duplicate content: two addresses serving one page, splitting inbound links and ranking signals between them. If only one resolves, every link that gets the case wrong is a 404, and people typing URLs get the case wrong constantly.

Standardising on lowercase removes the whole problem. Most content systems do this automatically; it is worth confirming yours does.

## Accents and other scripts

Accented Latin characters are straightforward: transliterate to the base letter. `café` becomes `cafe`, `Zürich` becomes `zurich`, `straße` becomes `strasse`.

The tool does this by Unicode NFD normalisation, which splits an accented letter into a base letter plus a combining mark, then discarding the marks. A few letters need explicit handling because they are genuinely distinct letters rather than decorated ones — `ß`, `ø`, `æ`, `ł` and `đ` do not decompose, so they are mapped by hand.

Non-Latin scripts are a genuine decision rather than a technical problem.

**Keeping the script** works. `https://example.com/日本語` is valid and modern browsers display it correctly. The cost is that the moment it leaves the browser it becomes `%E6%97%A5%E6%9C%AC%E8%AA%9E` — unreadable in an email, awkward in a printed document, and easy to corrupt when copied through systems that do not handle encoding well.

**Transliterating** produces an ASCII slug that travels anywhere, at the cost of being less readable to the audience it was written for.

For a site whose readers all share the script, keeping it is defensible. For anything likely to be shared as plain text, transliteration is safer. Whichever you choose, be consistent — a site with both is worse than a site with either.

Note that the domain is a separate matter. Non-ASCII domains are converted to punycode rather than percent-encoded, which is why `☕.example` becomes `xn--53h.example`.

## Stop words

Removing `the`, `of`, `and` and similar shortens a slug and raises the proportion of meaningful words. It is offered here as an option because sometimes that is right and sometimes it destroys the title.

```
"The Complete Guide to the History of Coffee"
  keep:   the-complete-guide-to-the-history-of-coffee
  strip:  complete-guide-history-coffee
```

That one improves. This one does not:

```
"The Who"        →  who
"War of the Worlds"  →  war-worlds
"To Kill a Mockingbird"  →  kill-mockingbird
```

The rule that works: strip stop words on long descriptive titles where they carry no information, and keep them wherever they are part of a name or change the meaning. The tool never returns an empty slug — if every word is a stop word, it keeps them all.

## Length

There is no search penalty for a long slug. There are three practical costs.

Long URLs get truncated in search results and social previews, so the end of a long slug is often invisible where it matters most. They are harder to read aloud, to type, and to paste into contexts that wrap lines. And an over-long slug is usually a sign the title is trying to do too much.

Three to five meaningful words is a good target. Front-load the distinctive ones: `slug-generator-free-online-tool` says the important part first, and `free-online-tool-slug-generator` buries it.

When the tool trims, it cuts at a word boundary rather than mid-word — `complete-guide-coff` is worse than nothing.

## Numbers and dates

**Numbers are fine** and often useful: `10-best-cafes-in-zurich`. They survive unencoded and they set expectations accurately.

**Dates are a trap.** `/2024/03/how-to-bake-bread` looks tidy and quietly commits you. The moment you update that article in 2026, either the URL contradicts the content or you change the URL and need a redirect. Unless the content is genuinely time-bound — news, an annual report, an event — leave the date out of the path and put it in the page.

The same logic applies to anything that might change: a year in the slug, a version number, a price. The slug should describe what the page is *about*, not what it currently says.

## Changing a slug after publishing

Expensive. Every inbound link, every bookmark, every ranking signal points at the old address.

If you must change it, put a **301 permanent redirect** from the old slug to the new one, and do it at the same moment as the change rather than afterwards. A 301 passes ranking signals to the new URL; a 404 discards them, and a few weeks of 404s is usually enough for the page to fall out of the index.

The practical implication is to get the slug right at publication and then leave it alone. It is worth thirty seconds of thought at the point where it is free.

## What the tool does

In order:

1. **Expand special letters** — `ß` to `ss`, `æ` to `ae`, `&` to `and`, `%` to `percent`, before anything else can strip them.
2. **Normalise and strip marks** — NFD decomposition, then remove the combining diacriticals block, turning `é` into `e`.
3. **Lowercase** everything.
4. **Split on anything that is not a letter or digit**, which discards punctuation, spaces and symbols in one step.
5. **Optionally drop stop words**, never to the point of emptiness.
6. **Join with the separator**, so repeated punctuation cannot produce a double hyphen.
7. **Trim to length at a word boundary** if a cap is set.

The result is checked against the pattern `^[a-z0-9]+([-_][a-z0-9]+)*$` — letters, digits and single separators only, no leading or trailing separator. If it passes, the slug needs no percent-encoding anywhere.
