## The three numbers this page produces

A calorie target is built from three figures stacked on top of each other, and knowing which is which explains most of what goes wrong with dieting.

**Basal metabolic rate** is what your body spends doing nothing at all — running your brain, heart, kidneys and liver, maintaining temperature, replacing cells. For most people it is 60 to 70 percent of the daily total. This page estimates it with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation:

```
Men:   BMR = 10·kg + 6.25·cm − 5·age + 5
Women: BMR = 10·kg + 6.25·cm − 5·age − 161
```

**Maintenance**, or total daily energy expenditure, is BMR multiplied by an activity factor between 1.2 and 1.9. It is the intake at which your weight holds still.

**Your target** is maintenance plus or minus whatever produces the rate of change you asked for. That is the number at the top of the calculator, and it is the only one you act on.

## Turning a rate into a daily figure

Body fat tissue carries roughly 7,700 calories per kilogram, or about 3,500 per pound. Losing half a kilogram a week therefore implies a shortfall of:

```
0.5 kg × 7,700 kcal ÷ 7 days = 550 kcal per day
```

That is the arithmetic, and it is where nearly every calorie calculator stops.

## Why the 3,500 rule stops working

The rule assumes your energy expenditure stays where it started while your body gets smaller. It does not.

Three things change as you lose weight. A lighter body costs less to maintain at rest, so BMR falls. A lighter body costs less to move, so the same walk burns less. And sustained restriction produces adaptive thermogenesis — a reduction in expenditure beyond what the weight change alone accounts for.

Kevin Hall's work at the NIH quantified this and produced the dynamic model behind the NIH Body Weight Planner. The practical finding is that the linear rule progressively overestimates loss: a deficit that delivers a pound a week in month one delivers noticeably less by month six, with no change in behaviour at all. In 2012 a joint consensus statement from the American Society for Nutrition and the International Life Sciences Institute recommended that practitioners abandon the 3,500-calorie rule of thumb in favour of dynamic models.

This page still uses the linear figure to set the initial target, because for the first several weeks it is close and because it is the only calculation you can check by hand. What it also does is show you a discounted estimate for the first month, and tell you plainly that the number needs resetting as your weight moves.

The behavioural consequence matters more than the arithmetic: **a plateau at month three is usually the deficit having closed, not a metabolic mystery.** Recalculate at your current weight and the target moves down with you.

## How fast is safe

The CDC puts steady, sustainable loss at 1 to 2 pounds — roughly 0.5 to 1 kg — a week, and notes that people losing at that pace are more likely to keep it off than people losing faster.

A better rule for individuals is proportional: **cap the rate at about 1 percent of body weight per week.** A fixed target of 1 kg a week is a gentle 0.8 percent for someone at 120 kg and an aggressive 1.8 percent for someone at 55 kg. The same absolute number describes completely different levels of stress on two different bodies, and the proportional version is what this calculator warns against exceeding.

Above roughly 1 percent per week, the additional loss comes disproportionately from lean tissue. You lose weight faster and a larger share of what leaves is muscle — which lowers your maintenance further, making the next phase harder.

For gaining, the ceiling is lower still. Muscle accrual is limited by training and recovery, not by intake, so a surplus beyond about 250 to 500 calories a day mostly adds fat. Gaining faster than roughly 0.25 to 0.5 percent of body weight per week is generally buying fat you will later have to lose.

## Why a target below BMR is a bad setting

The calculator flags any target that falls below your basal metabolic rate, and the reason is practical rather than moralistic.

At that intake the deficit is large enough that a meaningful share of it gets met by breaking down lean tissue rather than fat. And the calorie budget left over is too small to comfortably hold adequate protein, and to fit the volume of food required to reach normal vitamin and mineral intakes. Very low calorie diets are a real clinical tool with real indications — they are also delivered under supervision with monitoring, which is not what a slider on a web page provides.

If the calculator flags this, the fix is to pick a slower rate. The slower rate almost always reaches the same place, because it is the one you can actually hold.

## Do not eat back your exercise calories

This is the single most common way to accidentally erase a deficit.

The activity multiplier you selected already includes your typical exercise. Choosing "moderately active" and then adding back the 600 calories a fitness watch reported for a run counts the same run twice.

Consumer devices also overestimate exercise energy expenditure substantially — the error is large enough that a session's reported burn can be roughly double what actually happened. Combine double-counting with overestimation and a 500-calorie deficit disappears inside one snack.

Pick the multiplier that describes your normal week, leave it alone, and let the scale tell you over a month whether the estimate was right.

## What the deficit is made of

Total calories determine whether you lose weight. Protein and training determine how much of what you lose is fat rather than muscle.

Two things protect lean mass in a deficit, and both are well supported: adequate protein intake, and resistance training. Someone in a 500-calorie deficit who lifts twice a week and eats sufficient protein, and someone in the same deficit who does neither, will see the same number on the scale after three months and will be in noticeably different physical condition.

That is why the body composition question matters more than the weight question, and why weighing yourself is a poor sole measure of whether a diet is working.

## How accurate is any of this

Mifflin-St Jeor predicts resting energy expenditure within 10 percent for roughly 80 percent of people. That is the best-validated of the common equations, and it still means your true BMR could be 200 calories either side of the estimate.

The activity multiplier is far rougher than that. The gap between 1.375 and 1.55 is around 200 calories a day for an average adult, and most people select one bracket too high — an hour of exercise is a small share of a 24-hour day, and the rest of the day is what the multiplier is really describing.

So the honest position is that the number this page gives you is a **starting hypothesis**, not a measurement.

## How to actually use it

1. Take the target and hold it for two to three weeks without adjusting.
2. Weigh yourself under consistent conditions and use the weekly average, not any single reading. Day-to-day water shifts are several times larger than a day's genuine fat change.
3. Compare the trend against what you predicted. If your weight is flat over three weeks, your real maintenance is lower than estimated — cut 200 and repeat. If it is falling faster than intended, add 200.
4. Recalculate the whole thing every 4 to 5 kg of change.

After a month of that, you have something no equation can give you: a maintenance figure measured on the actual person eating the actual food. Your own data beats the model quickly, and the model's job is only to get you close enough to start collecting it.
