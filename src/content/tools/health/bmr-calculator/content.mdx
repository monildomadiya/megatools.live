## What BMR measures

Basal metabolic rate is the energy your body spends doing nothing. Not resting on the sofa — nothing. Lying still, awake, fasted, in a comfortable room, with your body doing only what it must: circulating blood, breathing, running your brain, maintaining temperature, repairing and replacing cells.

For most people that accounts for somewhere between 60 and 70 percent of everything they burn in a day. It is the largest single component of energy expenditure by a wide margin, which is why it anchors every calorie calculation even though it describes complete inactivity.

The rest comes from three places: the energy cost of digesting food, roughly 10 percent of intake; deliberate exercise; and everything else you do while awake — walking, standing, fidgeting, carrying things. That last category varies more between individuals than exercise does, and it is where most of the real difference in daily burn comes from.

## BMR is not quite what most calculators give you

True basal metabolic rate is measured under strict laboratory conditions: the subject sleeps at the testing facility, is measured immediately on waking, has fasted for twelve hours, and is at complete physical and mental rest in a thermally neutral room.

Resting metabolic rate is measured under more relaxed conditions and comes out around 10 percent higher.

Nearly every equation labelled "BMR" online, including Mifflin-St Jeor, was fitted against RMR measurements rather than true BMR. This calculator is no exception, and it is worth knowing because it explains why two apparently authoritative sources can quote different numbers for the same person. The distinction rarely changes a practical decision, but it should not be hidden.

## The two equations

### Mifflin-St Jeor (1990)

Weight in kilograms, height in centimetres, age in years:

```
Men:   BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5
Women: BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161
```

Derived by Mifflin and colleagues from indirect calorimetry on 498 healthy subjects spanning a wide range of body sizes. The only thing that differs between the sexes is the constant on the end.

### Harris-Benedict, revised by Roza and Shizgal (1984)

```
Men:   BMR = 88.362 + 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A
Women: BMR = 447.593 + 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A
```

The original Harris-Benedict equations were published in 1918, derived from a sample that skewed young, lean, and physically active by modern standards. Roza and Shizgal refitted the coefficients in 1984 against a broader dataset. The revised version is the one worth using; the 1918 coefficients are still circulating on the web and overestimate for most people.

## Worked example

A 30-year-old man, 80 kg, 180 cm.

**Mifflin-St Jeor:**
- 10 × 80 = 800
- 6.25 × 180 = 1,125
- 5 × 30 = 150
- 800 + 1,125 − 150 + 5 = **1,780 kcal/day**

**Harris-Benedict (revised):**
- 13.397 × 80 = 1,071.76
- 4.799 × 180 = 863.82
- 5.677 × 30 = 170.31
- 88.362 + 1,071.76 + 863.82 − 170.31 = **1,853.6 kcal/day**

A gap of about 74 calories, or 4 percent. Small enough to ignore for most purposes, large enough to notice if you are tracking tightly — and a useful reminder that the precision of a four-digit answer is illusory.

And a 30-year-old woman, 65 kg, 165 cm:

| Equation | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Mifflin-St Jeor | 1,370 kcal/day |
| Harris-Benedict (revised) | 1,430 kcal/day |

## Which one to trust

Mifflin-St Jeor, on the evidence.

A 2005 systematic review by Frankenfield, Roth-Yousey and Compher in the *Journal of the American Dietetic Association* compared the commonly used equations against measured resting metabolic rate. Mifflin-St Jeor predicted within 10 percent of measured values more often than any other equation tested, and had the narrowest error range.

It is not close to perfect. Accuracy was about 82 percent overall, and it fell to roughly 70 percent in obese participants — meaning that for around three people in ten with obesity, the prediction was off by more than 10 percent in one direction or the other.

That is the best available general-purpose equation, and it still misses badly for a meaningful minority.

## Why your real BMR probably differs

Individual metabolic rates vary by roughly 10 to 15 percent either side of prediction, even between people identical in height, weight, age and sex. Several things drive that spread, and none of them appear in the formula.

**Body composition.** Muscle is more metabolically active at rest than fat. Two people at the same weight, one lean and muscular, one not, have measurably different basal rates — and weight-and-height equations cannot tell them apart. This is the single largest source of error.

**Thyroid function.** Thyroid hormones set the pace of resting metabolism directly. Hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism move BMR substantially in either direction.

**Genetics.** Some of the variation between individuals is simply inherited and not predictable from anthropometrics.

**Recent dieting history.** Sustained energy restriction lowers resting rate by more than the change in body size alone accounts for — adaptive thermogenesis. Someone six months into a deficit may sit well below their predicted rate.

**Temperature and illness.** Cold exposure raises it. Fever raises it sharply. Both are transient but real.

## From BMR to a daily calorie figure

BMR on its own is not something you can eat to. It describes a body at complete rest, and nobody spends a day that way.

The conventional next step is to multiply BMR by an activity factor to estimate total daily energy expenditure — the figure that actually matters for weight management. The multipliers in general use run from about 1.2 for a sedentary desk-bound day up to around 1.9 for heavy physical work combined with daily training.

It is worth being straight about where those multipliers come from: they are conventions inherited from the Harris-Benedict tradition rather than values derived from a single definitive study, and they are applied as broad brackets to a population that varies continuously. For the 1,780 BMR in the worked example above, choosing "moderately active" (×1.55) over "lightly active" (×1.375) changes the answer by about 310 calories a day — and there is no objective test telling you which bracket you belong in.

That uncertainty stacks on top of the 10-to-15 percent already present in the BMR estimate itself. A total daily figure carries meaningfully more error than the BMR it was built from — which is an argument for treating it as a starting hypothesis and adjusting from measured results, not for calculating it more precisely.

Most people also underestimate the non-exercise side of the equation. The difference between someone who walks to work, takes stairs, and is on their feet through the day and someone who is seated from morning to evening can exceed the calorie cost of a gym session, and it is the part the activity multiplier captures worst.

## Can you increase it?

Modestly, and mostly by building muscle. Lean tissue burns roughly 13 calories per kilogram per day at rest — which means gaining two kilograms of muscle, a genuinely hard year of training for most people, adds about 26 calories a day.

That is not nothing, but it is far less than the fitness-industry framing suggests. The metabolic case for resistance training is real and rests on other things: preserving lean mass during weight loss, the energy cost of the training itself, and improved insulin sensitivity. It does not rest on a transformed resting burn.

Meal timing, "metabolism-boosting" foods, and green tea extract do not move BMR to any degree worth calculating.

## What to do with the number

Use it as a baseline to test against reality rather than a target to hit.

The practical approach: take your estimated BMR, multiply by an activity factor to get an estimate of total daily energy expenditure, eat at that level consistently for two to three weeks while tracking weight, and adjust based on what actually happens. Your own response is better evidence than any equation, because it accounts for all the individual variation the equation cannot see.

Recalculate as your weight changes. A projection made at your starting weight becomes progressively wrong as you move away from it.

And do not treat BMR as a floor or a target for intake. It is an estimate of a physiological baseline, not dietary advice. Intake below it is a clinical decision, made with a doctor or a registered dietitian who knows your situation.
