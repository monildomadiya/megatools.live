## There is no such thing as an ideal weight

That is the honest headline, and it is worth stating before the formulas rather than after them.

Every equation on this page returns a single number to one decimal place. None of them was derived from a study of health outcomes. Three of the four were published as clinical dosing tools, and all four trace back to the same source: the height-weight tables that US life insurance companies compiled in the middle of the twentieth century from the policyholders who happened to buy their products.

The formulas are still worth showing, because they are what people are asking for and because seeing them disagree with each other is the fastest way to understand how much confidence they deserve. But the figure on this page that actually means something is the healthy BMI range, and it is a range on purpose.

## Where these equations came from

In 2000, Pai and Paloucek published a review in the *Annals of Pharmacotherapy* tracing the ideal body weight equations back to their origins. Their finding was that the equations agree with one another because they were all fitted to the same underlying tables — the agreement is shared ancestry, not independent confirmation of a biological truth.

### Hamwi (1964)

Devised as a bedside rule for estimating diabetic patients' calorie needs. It is the most generous of the four at tall heights, adding 2.7 kg for every inch above five feet.

```
Men:   48.0 kg + 2.7 kg per inch over 5 ft
Women: 45.5 kg + 2.2 kg per inch over 5 ft
```

### Devine (1974)

The most widely used, and the one most often mistaken for an authority. Devine published it in a paper about **gentamicin dosing**. Some drugs distribute through lean tissue rather than fat, so dosing an obese patient on total body weight overdoses them; the formula gave clinicians a lean-weight proxy to dose against.

```
Men:   50.0 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 ft
Women: 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 ft
```

It is still the standard in clinical pharmacy, for exactly the purpose it was built for. It was never validated as a health target, and it does not become one by being widely quoted.

### Robinson (1983) and Miller (1983)

Both were published in the same year as revisions of Devine, refitted to newer versions of the insurance tables. Miller in particular uses a much shallower slope — 1.41 kg per inch for men against Hamwi's 2.7.

```
Robinson  Men: 52.0 kg + 1.9 kg/in    Women: 49.0 kg + 1.7 kg/in
Miller    Men: 56.2 kg + 1.41 kg/in   Women: 53.1 kg + 1.36 kg/in
```

## Watch them diverge

At 5 ft 9 in the four formulas land within a few kilograms of each other, which is why they look interchangeable. Push the height out and the agreement collapses:

| Height (men) | Hamwi | Devine | Robinson | Miller |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 ft 2 in | 53.4 kg | 54.6 kg | 55.8 kg | 59.0 kg |
| 5 ft 9 in | 72.3 kg | 70.7 kg | 69.1 kg | 68.9 kg |
| 6 ft 4 in | 89.1 kg | 85.0 kg | 81.2 kg | 77.7 kg |

At 6 ft 4 in the spread is over 11 kg. The formulas are not measuring the same thing with small differences in precision; they are extrapolating different slopes out of the same cloud of mid-century data, and the further you get from the average height in that data the more the choice of formula dominates the answer.

Notice too that the ranking inverts. Miller gives the highest number for a short man and the lowest for a tall one. Any conclusion you draw from "the ideal weight formula says" depends on which one you happened to open.

## Below five feet they stop working

Every one of the four is defined as a base weight at exactly five feet plus a fixed amount per inch above it. Below five feet, the per-inch term goes negative and the equation runs backwards off the end of the data it was fitted to.

The results are not merely imprecise there — they are unsupported. For anyone under about 5 ft, only the BMI-derived figures on this page are worth reading.

## The healthy BMI range

This is the number to use. The World Health Organization classifies a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 as healthy weight for adults, and multiplying each end by your height in metres squared converts it into a weight range for you:

```
lower = 18.5 × height²      upper = 24.9 × height²
```

For someone 1.75 m tall that is 56.7 kg to 76.3 kg — a span of nearly 20 kg.

That width is not sloppiness. It reflects what the evidence actually supports: across that range, there is no demonstrated advantage to any particular weight. A formula that answers "70.7 kg" is not more accurate than a range of 56.7 to 76.3; it is the same uncertainty with the error bars deleted.

The BMI band has its own well-known limits — it cannot distinguish muscle from fat, and the thresholds sit lower for several Asian populations, where WHO added public health action points at 23.0 and 27.5. But unlike the insurance tables, it was chosen against mortality and morbidity data.

## The universal equation

Peterson and colleagues published a replacement in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* in 2016 that avoids the structural problems of the older formulas:

```
weight = 2.2 × BMI + 3.5 × BMI × (height in metres − 1.5)
```

Three things make it better. It is valid at any height rather than only above five feet. It uses a single equation for both sexes. And it takes the target BMI as an input, which makes the choice explicit instead of burying someone else's assumption inside a constant.

That last point is the important one. "Ideal weight" always encodes a target — the older formulas just do not tell you what theirs is. Setting it yourself, and knowing that 22 sits in the middle of the healthy band, is more honest than accepting a number whose provenance is an insurance actuary's table from 1959.

## What height-based formulas cannot see

Height is the only input any of these equations has. Everything else that determines a healthy weight for a specific person is invisible to them:

**Muscle mass.** A rugby forward and a sedentary man of the same height receive identical answers. One of them is carrying 15 kg of muscle the formula has no way to account for.

**Frame size.** Skeletal width varies enough between people of the same height to move a genuinely healthy weight by several kilograms in either direction.

**Fat distribution.** Two people at the same weight with the same height can have very different amounts of visceral fat, which is the fat that actually predicts metabolic risk. Waist circumference sees this; height does not.

**Age.** None of the formulas has an age term. Adults lose muscle mass from roughly their forties onward, so holding a constant weight across decades usually means slowly trading muscle for fat while the number on the scale reports no change at all.

## How to use this page

Read the healthy BMI range as the answer, and the four formulas as a demonstration of why a single number was never available.

If you want one figure to aim at, take the midpoint of the healthy range, or set the universal equation to a target BMI of 22. Then stop optimising the number: whether you are at 68 kg or 73 kg matters considerably less than your body composition, your fitness, and whether the weight is stable.

If your build is unusual in either direction, a body fat measurement and a waist circumference will tell you more in five minutes than any amount of arguing with a height-based formula.
