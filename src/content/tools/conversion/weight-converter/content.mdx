## Mass, not weight

The page is called a weight converter because that is what people search for. What it actually converts is mass, and the distinction is worth thirty seconds.

**Mass** is how much matter something contains. It does not change with location.

**Weight** is the force gravity exerts on that mass. It changes with location — the same object weighs about a sixth as much on the Moon, and measurably less at the equator than at the poles.

Kilograms, pounds, stone, ounces and tons are all units of mass. The SI unit of force is the newton, and the customary unit is the pound-force. So a bathroom scale, which reports kilograms or pounds, is reporting a mass, and "how much do you weigh" is technically asking for one.

In ordinary use this never matters. It matters in engineering, and it matters if you have ever wondered why a physicist got pedantic about it.

## The kilogram since 2019

Until 2019 the kilogram was the last SI base unit defined by a physical object: a platinum-iridium cylinder made in 1889 and kept in a vault near Paris. Its official copies, distributed to national laboratories, were periodically compared against it.

Over a century they drifted apart by tens of micrograms. Nobody could say whether the copies had gained mass or the prototype had lost it, because the prototype *was* the definition — it weighed exactly one kilogram by fiat, whatever happened to it.

In May 2019 the definition changed. The kilogram is now fixed by defining the Planck constant as exactly 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ joule seconds, which pins mass to invariants of nature via the second and the metre. Any suitably equipped laboratory can now realise a kilogram without reference to any object.

Nothing changed by an amount you could measure on a kitchen scale. The point was to remove the dependence on a single artefact that could be scratched, contaminated, or dropped.

## Where the imperial numbers come from

Every imperial and US customary factor on this page is **exact**, not measured, and they became exact on the same day.

The 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement, signed by the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa, defined:

```
1 international pound = 0.45359237 kilograms   exactly
```

Everything else in the system is a whole-number multiple or division of that pound: sixteen ounces to the pound, fourteen pounds to the stone, seven thousand grains to the pound. So each conversion factor is an exact rational number, and any decimal you see is only a rounding for display.

## Stone

Fourteen pounds, still in daily use for body weight in the UK and Ireland and essentially unknown everywhere else — including in the US, which otherwise shares the pound.

The convention that catches people out is that stone is never quoted as a decimal. Body weight is given as stone **and** pounds:

```
"11 stone 4"  =  11 × 14 + 4  =  158 lb  =  71.7 kg
```

Nobody says "11.3 stone". The calculator shows the stone-and-pounds form separately for this reason — the decimal figure in the table is arithmetically correct and conversationally wrong.

## Troy against avoirdupois

Two parallel systems, both descended from the grain, both still in use.

**Avoirdupois** is the ordinary one: 16 ounces to the pound, 7,000 grains to the pound.

**Troy** is used for precious metals: 12 ounces to the pound, 5,760 grains to the pound.

The result is a genuinely confusing inversion:

```
Troy ounce      31.103 g   >   avoirdupois ounce   28.350 g    (about 10% heavier)
Troy pound     373.242 g   <   avoirdupois pound  453.592 g    (about 18% lighter)
```

A troy ounce is heavier, and a troy pound is lighter, because there are fewer of the heavier ounces in it.

This is not trivia if you are buying gold. Bullion is priced per troy ounce, so a "spot price per ounce" quoted against a mass measured on a kitchen scale in ordinary ounces overstates the quantity by about 10 percent. Gold, silver, platinum and palladium are all traded in troy ounces; nothing else is.

## Three kinds of ton

The most consequential ambiguity in this whole system, because all three are commonly just called "a ton".

```
US short ton    2,000 lb    907.18 kg    ~9% lighter than a tonne
Metric tonne                1,000 kg
UK long ton     2,240 lb  1,016.05 kg    ~1.6% heavier than a tonne
```

The metric tonne and the long ton are within 2 percent of each other, which is close enough that the difference hides easily in shipping and commodity figures. The short ton is nearly 10 percent off, which is not.

Conventions by region: "ton" in the US means the short ton. "Tonne" anywhere means the metric tonne. British usage has largely moved to the tonne, but historical UK figures — shipping tonnage, coal output, older engineering documents — mean the long ton.

When a figure matters, the unit needs stating. "Fifty thousand tons" is a range spanning nearly 5,000 tonnes depending on which was meant.

## Grains

Exactly 1/7000 of a pound, about 64.8 milligrams, and the shared ancestor of both systems. It survives in two places.

**Ammunition.** Bullet and propellant masses are given in grains — a 9 mm round might use a 115-grain bullet. The unit is small enough to make meaningful distinctions without decimals.

**Pharmacy.** Some older preparations are still dosed in grains, which is a known hazard: a grain is about 64.8 mg, and the historical rounding to 60 mg or 65 mg has caused dosing errors. Modern practice is metric for exactly this reason.

## Worth memorising

**1 kg ≈ 2.2 lb.** For a quick mental conversion, double and add 10 percent: 70 kg → 140 → 154 lb. The true answer is 154.3.

**1 lb ≈ 0.45 kg.** Halve and take off a tenth: 150 lb → 75 → 67.5 kg. True answer 68.0.

**1 oz ≈ 28 g.** Close enough for cooking, where the recipe tolerance is wider than the rounding.

**1 stone ≈ 6.35 kg.** Or: your weight in stone is roughly your weight in kg divided by 6.35.

## Why recipes in grams beat recipes in cups

The most common everyday use of this converter is cooking, and it is worth knowing why one system is measurably better than the other.

A gram is a mass. A cup is a volume. Converting between them requires knowing the density of the specific ingredient, as it is packed, on the day.

Flour is the notorious case. A cup of flour scooped straight from the bag compacts against the sides and can hold 20 to 25 percent more than a cup spooned in and levelled. That is the difference between a tender crumb and a dense one, and no amount of careful measuring fixes it, because the cup is measuring the wrong quantity.

Mass has no such problem. 120 grams of flour is 120 grams of flour whether it is packed, sifted, or poured from height.

This is why professional and most European recipes are written by mass, and why the useful conversion for a US recipe is not "cups to grams" in general — there is no such conversion — but "cups of this specific ingredient to grams", which needs a per-ingredient table.

The units this page handles are all masses, so every conversion here is exact and unambiguous. The moment volume enters, exactness leaves.

## Why everything routes through kilograms

The calculator converts your value into kilograms and then out to the target, rather than holding a table of direct conversions between every pair.

With eleven units, a pair-to-pair table needs 110 entries, each one a rounded decimal. Converting grains to long tons through such a table means multiplying by a rounded number, and chaining several such steps accumulates error.

Going through a single base unit uses exactly two factors per conversion, and both of them are exact. The result is limited only by the floating-point arithmetic itself, which is far below the precision anyone displays.
