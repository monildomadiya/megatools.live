## What an EMI is

An Equated Monthly Instalment is a fixed monthly payment sized so that a loan reaches exactly zero at the end of its term. Every instalment covers the interest that accrued that month and puts whatever is left toward the principal.

The term is standard across India and increasingly used elsewhere. In the US and UK the same arrangement is usually just called the monthly payment on an amortising loan. The arithmetic does not change with the name.

What does change over the life of the loan is what your fixed payment is buying. Interest is charged on what you still owe, and what you owe is highest at the start — so early instalments are mostly interest and late ones are mostly principal, even though the amount leaving your account never moves.

## The formula

```
EMI = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ / ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)
```

**P** is the loan amount, **r** is the monthly interest rate — the annual rate divided by 12 and by 100 — and **n** is the number of monthly instalments.

If the rate is zero, this formula divides by zero and the answer is simply P ÷ n. Interest-free loans are real (family arrangements, 0 percent retail finance), and the calculator handles that case separately.

## Worked example

A 25,000 loan at 11 percent annual interest over 5 years.

- Monthly rate: 11 ÷ 100 ÷ 12 = 0.0091667
- Instalments: 5 × 12 = 60
- (1 + r)ⁿ = 1.0091667⁶⁰ = 1.7289
- EMI = 25,000 × 0.0091667 × 1.7289 ÷ (1.7289 − 1)
- EMI = **543.56 a month**

Over 60 instalments that totals 32,613.63, of which **7,613.63 is interest** — about 23 percent of everything you pay.

The first instalment breaks down as 229.17 interest and 314.39 principal. By the final instalment those are roughly reversed.

## Term length is the biggest lever

Same loan, same rate, different terms:

| Term | Monthly instalment | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 years | 818.47 | 4,464.85 |
| 5 years | 543.56 | 7,613.63 |

Stretching from three years to five cuts the monthly payment by about a third and increases the interest by 70 percent. Neither is automatically the right choice — a payment you can comfortably meet in a bad month has real value — but the trade should be made deliberately rather than by accepting whatever term the lender leads with.

Lenders often default to the longest term available, because a lower monthly figure is easier to sell.

## Flat rate versus reducing balance

This matters more than almost anything else on this page, and it is where borrowers are most often caught out.

**Reducing balance** charges interest on what you still owe. As you repay, the interest charge falls. This is how mortgages work, how most regulated personal lending works, and how every APR disclosure is calculated. It is what this calculator uses.

**Flat rate** charges interest on the original loan amount for the entire term, regardless of how much you have repaid. Ten percent flat on 25,000 over five years means 2,500 of interest every year for five years — 12,500 — even though your average balance across the term is barely half the original amount.

The gap is not small. A 10 percent flat rate over five years is equivalent to about **17.3 percent** on a reducing balance. Over three years it works out at about **17.9 percent**.

If a lender quotes a flat rate, the number to ask for is the APR. In many jurisdictions they are required to disclose it.

## Overpaying

Any payment above the instalment goes straight against the principal, and that amount then stops accruing interest for every remaining month of the term. The saving is therefore much larger than the overpayment itself.

On the 25,000 example at 11 percent over five years:

| Extra per month | Loan clears in | Interest paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nothing | 60 months | 7,613.63 |
| 50 | 54 months | 6,738.03 |
| 100 | 49 months | 6,046.86 |

An extra 100 a month — 4,900 in total overpayments — removes 1,566.77 of interest and eleven months of debt.

Three things to check before doing it:

**Early repayment charges.** Common on fixed-rate products, and they can exceed the interest saved on a loan with a short remaining term.

**How the lender applies it.** Some hold extra payments as prepaid future instalments rather than reducing the principal. Those are not the same thing, and only one of them saves you interest.

**Term versus instalment.** By default an overpayment shortens the term. Some lenders will instead recalculate the instalment downward and keep the term — which saves far less. Say which you want.

## The rate you are quoted is not the rate you get

Advertised loan rates carry a qualifier that is easy to miss: most lenders are only required to offer the headline rate to a proportion of successful applicants — 51 percent in the UK, where such rates are labelled "representative APR". The other 49 percent can legitimately be offered something higher after their application is assessed.

What moves your rate is largely your credit file, your income relative to existing commitments, and the loan purpose. Secured loans price lower than unsecured. Car loans on new vehicles price lower than on used ones, because the collateral is worth more and is easier to value.

Two practical consequences.

**Check your credit report before applying, not after.** Errors are common and take weeks to correct. An incorrect default or an unclosed account you thought was settled can move your rate by several percentage points.

**Understand what a search does.** A quotation search — sometimes called a soft search — shows you an indicative rate without leaving a mark visible to other lenders. A full application leaves a hard search that other lenders can see, and several in quick succession reads as distress. Use eligibility checkers that do soft searches while you are comparing, and make one full application when you have decided.

If the rate you are actually offered differs materially from the one you modelled here, re-run the numbers before accepting. A two-point difference on a five-year loan is not a rounding error.

## Interest rate is not APR

The interest rate drives the instalment. The APR bundles in mandatory fees — processing charges, arrangement fees, compulsory insurance — and expresses the whole cost as an annual rate.

Two loans advertising the same rate can have materially different APRs, and the one with the lower headline rate is not reliably the cheaper loan. Compare APRs.

## What this calculator does not include

**Fees.** Processing and arrangement fees are often deducted from the disbursed amount, so you receive less than the loan amount but pay interest on all of it.

**Bundled insurance.** Credit life or payment protection policies added to the principal increase both the instalment and the total interest.

**Interest from the disbursement date.** Many lenders charge interest from the day funds are released, not from the first instalment date. If those dates are weeks apart, there is a partial period of interest at the start that no standard schedule shows.

**Variable rates.** The formula assumes the rate holds for the full term. On a floating-rate loan, a rate change alters either the instalment or the term, depending on the product.

**Missed payments.** Interest keeps accruing on an unpaid balance and most lenders add a fee. If a payment is short enough that it no longer covers the accrued interest, the balance grows instead of shrinking — negative amortisation. Speak to the lender before a payment is missed rather than after.

Use this to compare structures against each other. Use the lender's binding offer, and its APR, to decide.
