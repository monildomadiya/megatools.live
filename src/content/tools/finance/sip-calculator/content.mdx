## What a SIP is

A Systematic Investment Plan is not a product. It is an instruction: take a fixed amount from my account on the same date each month and buy units of this mutual fund with it.

That distinction matters because "SIP returns" is a phrase with no meaning. The returns belong to the fund. The SIP is only the schedule on which you buy into it, and what it changes is not what you earn but *when* you buy and *whether you keep buying*.

## The arithmetic

Every instalment is its own investment, compounding from the day it lands until maturity. The first one has the full term to grow; the last one has a month. The total is the sum of that series:

```
FV = P × [((1 + i)ⁿ − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i)
```

where `P` is the monthly instalment, `i` is the annual return divided by twelve, and `n` is the number of instalments.

The trailing `(1 + i)` is the part people leave out. It is there because a SIP debit happens at the *start* of the month, so every instalment earns one extra month of growth compared with the standard end-of-period annuity. Over a long term it is worth roughly one month's return on the whole corpus — small as a percentage, large in absolute terms.

This page does not use the closed form directly. It simulates month by month, because the step-up option changes the instalment every twelfth month and the closed form only handles a constant one.

### A worked example

₹10,000 a month, 12 percent assumed annual return, 15 years:

```
i = 0.12 ÷ 12 = 0.01
n = 180

FV = 10,000 × [((1.01)¹⁸⁰ − 1) ÷ 0.01] × 1.01 ≈ ₹50.5 lakh
```

You put in ₹18 lakh. The remaining ₹32.5 lakh is growth — about 64 percent of the final amount. That ratio is the single most useful output of a SIP projection, and it climbs steeply with the term. At 25 years the same instalment produces a corpus where growth is over 80 percent of the total.

## Rupee cost averaging

Because the instalment is a fixed amount of money rather than a fixed number of units, a falling market buys more units and a rising market buys fewer. Over many purchases the average cost per unit ends up below the average price over the same period.

This is a real effect and it is worth understanding precisely, because it is routinely oversold.

**What it does:** removes the need to time your entry. You are not betting the outcome of a decade on the level of the index on the day your bonus arrived.

**What it does not do:** protect you from a falling market. If the fund is worth less at the end than the average price you paid, averaging your entry does not save you. A SIP that ran through a decade-long bear market ends underwater, and no amount of averaging changes that.

The honest framing is that rupee cost averaging manages **timing** risk, not **market** risk.

## The step-up is the biggest lever you control

You cannot control returns. You can control the instalment, and increasing it annually does more than almost anything else available to you.

The reason is where the increases land. A step-up raises the amount in year two, and that raise then compounds for the remaining eighteen years of a twenty-year plan. Compare over a 20-year term at 12 percent:

| Plan | Total invested | Final corpus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ₹10,000/month flat | ₹24 lakh | about ₹1.0 crore |
| ₹10,000/month, +10% a year | ₹68.7 lakh | about ₹2.0 crore |

Read those two columns against each other, because the relationship between them is the actual lesson. Contributions went up by a factor of 2.9; the corpus only doubled. Money added in year fifteen has five years to compound, not twenty, so later rupees are worth considerably less than early ones.

That cuts both ways. A step-up is the most powerful lever you control, and it is still weaker than simply starting earlier. If you can only do one, start.

Because most people's incomes rise over their working life, a flat SIP also quietly shrinks as a share of income every year. A 10 percent step-up roughly tracks a normal [salary](/tools/finance/salary-calculator) trajectory — it is closer to standing still than to trying harder.

## SIP against lump sum

These are usually presented as rival strategies. They answer different questions.

**If you already hold the money**, the historical evidence generally favours investing it at once. Markets rise more often than they fall, so cash waiting on the sidelines spends most of its time missing returns. Spreading a lump sum over twelve months lowers the variance of the outcome and, on average, lowers the outcome.

**If the money does not exist yet** — which is the situation for most people, most of the time — there is no lump sum to compare against. You are investing income as it arrives, and the SIP is simply the mechanism for doing that without a monthly decision.

That last point is the real advantage, and it is behavioural rather than mathematical. An automated instalment keeps buying during the months when a human would not: the ones where the market is down and the news is bad, which are historically the most valuable months to be buying.

## The return assumption dominates everything

The calculator shows the same plan at three rates for a reason. Over long horizons, small differences in assumed return produce enormous differences in outcome, and a figure quoted to the rupee at a single assumed rate reads as a forecast when it is nothing of the sort.

Equity returns are not a rate you are offered. They are the realised outcome of a market over a period that has not happened yet. Twelve percent is a commonly used planning figure; it is not a promise, and years exist in which the fund loses value.

The practical test: look at the low column. **If your goal is only reached at the top of the range, the plan does not work.** Either lengthen the term, raise the instalment, or reduce the goal — those are the three inputs you actually control.

## Inflation is the number that matters

A projection quoting a maturity value in future rupees flatters itself. At 5 percent inflation, money loses roughly half its purchasing power every fourteen years.

That is why the calculator reports what the corpus is worth in today's money. A ₹1 crore projection over 20 years buys what about ₹37 lakh buys now. Neither figure is wrong; the second is the one that tells you whether the plan reaches a real goal like a house or an education.

Set your target in today's money, and compare it against the inflation-adjusted line rather than the headline.

## What the projection leaves out

**Expense ratios.** Fund charges are deducted from returns before you see them. A 1 percent expense ratio on a 12 percent gross return leaves 11 percent, and over 20 years that gap is large. Entering a rate net of expenses gets you closer to reality.

**Tax.** Capital gains tax applies on redemption in most jurisdictions, at rates that depend on the fund type and holding period. The corpus you can spend is smaller than the corpus you accumulate.

**Sequence of returns.** The calculator applies a smooth monthly rate. Real returns arrive in a jagged order, and the order matters — particularly near the end of the term, when a bad year hits the largest balance you will ever hold. Two funds with identical average returns over 20 years can deliver noticeably different final amounts depending on when the bad years fell.

**Exit loads and lock-ins.** Some funds charge for early redemption; ELSS funds lock each instalment for its own term from its own date, so a SIP into one does not become fully liquid until three years after the final instalment.

## The cost of stopping early

Stopping a SIP carries no penalty in most funds. It carries an opportunity cost, and the cost is heavily front-loaded.

An instalment made in year one of a twenty-year plan has nineteen years to compound. One made in year eighteen has two. Skipping a year at the start of a plan costs several times what skipping a year near the end does, even though both are the same amount of money.

If cash is tight, reducing the instalment is almost always better than pausing it — the schedule is the part that is hard to restart, not the amount.
