## What this converts, and what it deliberately does not

Give it a figure and the period it is paid over, and it produces every other period plus an annual total. That is useful for comparing two offers quoted in different units, which is the situation almost everyone is in when they open a page like this.

Every number here is **gross**. Nothing on this page knows your tax residency, filing status, pension contributions, or which of your benefits are salary-sacrificed. A calculator that claimed to show your take-home without knowing any of those would be confidently wrong, which is worse than being silent.

## The 2,080-hour convention

The standard shortcut for hourly-to-annual is to multiply by 2,080:

```
40 hours × 52 weeks = 2,080 hours
$25 × 2,080 = $52,000
```

It is worth knowing that this is a convention rather than a measurement.

No calendar year contains exactly 52 weeks — 365 days is 52 weeks and one day, and leap years add another. More importantly, 2,080 counts every week as worked, including the ones you spend on holiday. For a salaried employee that is correct, because paid leave is paid. For an hourly worker without paid holiday it is not.

The convention persists because it makes offers comparable. Just do not mistake it for a description of your year.

## Biweekly is not semi-monthly

These sound interchangeable and are not, and the difference shows up in your bank account rather than in your annual total.

| | Payments per year | On $52,000 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biweekly — every two weeks | 26 | $2,000.00 |
| Semi-monthly — twice a month | 24 | $2,166.67 |

The annual figure is identical. What changes is the rhythm.

Twenty-six fortnightly payments do not divide evenly into twelve months, so **twice a year you receive three cheques in one calendar month**. That third cheque feels like a windfall and is not — it is the same salary, and the other ten months are correspondingly lighter than a monthly figure suggests.

The budgeting error this produces is common and specific: dividing your annual salary by twelve, treating that as monthly income, and then finding that most months come up short because two biweekly cheques is 7.7 percent less than a month's pay. If you are paid biweekly, budget on two cheques a month and treat the third as the buffer it actually is.

## Paid weeks: where an hourly rate quietly loses

This is the field that changes the answer most, and it is the one most calculators do not offer.

Consider $25 an hour, 40 hours a week:

```
52 paid weeks:  $52,000
48 paid weeks:  $48,000     — four unpaid weeks off
44 paid weeks:  $44,000     — plus a slow month
```

A salaried employee at $52,000 who takes four weeks of paid holiday still receives $52,000. An hourly worker at the same nominal rate who takes the same four weeks receives $48,000 — around 8 percent less for the same work at the same rate.

That is the entire mechanism behind contractor rates. A contract paying 20 percent more per hour with no paid holiday, no sick pay, no employer pension contribution and no notice period is frequently worth **less** than the salaried alternative once you set the paid weeks to what you will actually be paid for.

To compare properly: set paid weeks realistically for each option, take the annual figures, and then add the employer's pension contribution and the cash value of paid leave to the salaried side.

## Overtime under the FLSA

In the United States, the Fair Labor Standards Act requires non-exempt employees to be paid at least **one and a half times their regular rate for hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek**.

Three details matter more than the headline.

### The workweek is the unit

Overtime is computed per workweek — a fixed, recurring period of 168 hours. It does not average across two weeks. Fifty hours one week and thirty the next is ten hours of overtime, not a balanced eighty.

The FLSA also sets **no daily overtime requirement**. Working twelve hours in a day triggers nothing federally, as long as the week stays under 40. Several states impose their own daily rules, and where state law is more generous, state law applies.

### The regular rate is not your base rate

This is the most commonly misunderstood part. The regular rate is:

```
total compensation for the workweek ÷ total hours actually worked
```

Non-discretionary bonuses, shift differentials, and production bonuses are part of total compensation, so they raise the regular rate — and therefore raise the overtime premium owed on the hours already worked that week. An employer paying overtime on base rate alone while also paying a production bonus is underpaying.

### Salaried does not mean exempt

The Department of Labor's own example makes the point: an employee hired at a weekly salary of $405 for a 45-hour week has a regular rate of $405 ÷ 45 = $9.00. The salary already covered straight time for all 45 hours, so what is still owed is the extra half-time on the five overtime hours — $4.50 × 5 = $22.50.

Exemption depends on the duties performed and the salary level, not on whether pay arrives as a salary or on what the job title says. A salaried employee who does not meet an exemption test is entitled to overtime.

## Comparing two offers properly

The annual figure is the right basis for comparison, but it is not the whole comparison. Things the conversion cannot see:

**Employer pension contribution.** Often 3 to 10 percent of salary, and it is real money.

**Paid leave.** Convert it: 25 days of paid holiday on a 5-day week is five weeks, worth roughly 10 percent of an annual salary compared with an offer that has none.

**Sick pay and notice periods.** Both are insurance. Their value is invisible right up to the moment you need them.

**Hours actually worked.** A $90,000 role at 55 hours a week pays $31 an hour against 2,860 hours. An $80,000 role at 40 pays $38 against 2,080. The lower salary is the higher rate, and the conversion only reveals it if you enter the hours honestly.

That last one is why the hours-per-week field is worth filling in accurately rather than putting 40 in reflexively.

## Gross is not net

The gap between the two is large and varies enormously. Income tax, social security or national insurance, pension contributions, student loan repayments and benefit deductions all come out before the money reaches you, and the effective rate depends on where you live, how much you earn, and your personal circumstances.

Two consequences worth holding onto.

First, **a raise is not worth its face value.** A $5,000 increase at a 40 percent combined marginal rate is $3,000 in hand. This is not an argument against raises — it is an argument for evaluating them post-tax rather than being disappointed later.

Second, **comparing gross across borders is close to meaningless.** Two countries' headline salaries can differ by 30 percent while take-home differs by five, or the other way round. Compare net figures using a calculator built for the specific jurisdiction, and treat the numbers on this page as the input to that step rather than the answer.
