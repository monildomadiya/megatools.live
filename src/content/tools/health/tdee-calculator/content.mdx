## What TDEE is

Total daily energy expenditure is everything your body burns in twenty-four hours. It is the number people usually mean when they ask how many calories they need, and it breaks into four parts.

**Basal metabolic rate** — the energy spent staying alive at complete rest. Typically 60 to 70 percent of the total, and by far the largest component. The [BMR calculator](/tools/health/bmr-calculator) covers this in detail.

**The thermic effect of food** — the energy used digesting and absorbing what you eat. Roughly 10 percent of intake, and higher for protein than for fat or carbohydrate.

**Exercise** — deliberate training. Usually a smaller share than people expect. An hour of moderate running might be 500 to 700 calories against a daily total in the thousands.

**Everything else you do while awake** — walking, standing, cooking, carrying shopping, fidgeting. This category, sometimes called non-exercise activity thermogenesis, varies more between individuals than exercise does, and it is where most of the genuine difference in daily burn comes from.

## How it is calculated

```
TDEE = BMR × physical activity level
```

This calculator computes BMR with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation and multiplies by the factor you select.

Worth being clear about where the uncertainty sits. The BMR half is well validated — it predicts within 10 percent of measured values for roughly 82 percent of people. The multiplier is a bracket chosen by self-assessment from a list of vague descriptions, and it carries far more error than the equation it multiplies.

## Worked example

A 30-year-old man, 80 kg, 180 cm, moderately active.

- Mifflin-St Jeor BMR: 10(80) + 6.25(180) − 5(30) + 5 = **1,780 kcal/day**
- Moderately active multiplier: 1.55
- TDEE: 1,780 × 1.55 = **2,759 kcal/day**

Straightforward. And, on the official evidence, probably too low.

## The multiplier problem

Here is something most TDEE calculators do not mention: there are two different sets of activity numbers in circulation, they disagree substantially, and only one of them comes from a body that measured anything.

The familiar set — 1.2 for sedentary rising to 1.9 for extra active — comes out of the Harris-Benedict tradition and propagated through fitness writing. It is everywhere, and it is difficult to trace to a primary study.

The 2001 Joint FAO/WHO/UNU Expert Consultation on Human Energy Requirements measured physical activity level directly across populations and published ranges rather than single values:

| Lifestyle | FAO/WHO/UNU PAL | Common multiplier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sedentary or light activity | 1.40 – 1.69 | 1.2 – 1.375 |
| Active or moderately active | 1.70 – 1.99 | 1.55 – 1.725 |
| Vigorously active | 2.00 – 2.40 | 1.9 |

Look at the first row. The FAO floor for a sedentary lifestyle is **1.40**. The common set puts sedentary at **1.2** — below the official range entirely.

For the worked example, that is the difference between 2,136 calories and 2,492 for the same sedentary man. Over a week, 2,500 calories of disagreement, from nothing but which table someone copied.

The calculator shows both. Where they diverge, the gap is a reasonable measure of how much confidence the number deserves — which is less than a four-digit answer implies.

## Choosing a level

Most people choose one bracket too high, and the reason is a reasonable intuition applied wrongly: a hard workout feels significant, so it seems like it should move a daily total significantly.

Do the arithmetic. A one-hour session at 600 calories, four times a week, is 2,400 calories spread over seven days — about 340 a day. That is roughly one bracket, not three. Someone with a desk job who trains four times a week is light-to-moderate, not "very active".

"Extra active" means a physically demanding job — construction, farm work, professional sport — *plus* training. It does not mean an office worker who goes hard at the gym.

There is a better method than any of this, if your weight has been stable for a month or more: **your current intake is your actual TDEE.** No multiplier can compete with that, because it already accounts for every individual factor the equation cannot see. Use the calculator to sanity-check that figure, not to override it.

## Using it for weight change

The calculator shows deficits as calorie changes rather than as promised weekly weight loss, and that is deliberate.

The familiar rule says a pound of fat stores 3,500 calories, so a 500-a-day deficit loses a pound a week. It works for the first few weeks and then progressively overstates, for two compounding reasons. A lighter body burns less, so the same intake represents a shrinking deficit. And sustained restriction produces adaptive thermogenesis — metabolic rate falls by more than the size change alone accounts for.

Modelling published by Hall and colleagues in *The Lancet* in 2011 showed that body weight responds to a sustained change in intake over a timescale of about a year, approaching a new equilibrium rather than declining at a fixed weekly rate. A linear projection from the 3,500-calorie rule diverges from reality further the longer you run it.

Practically: expect the early weeks to roughly track the rule, and expect later weeks to fall short of it. That is the system working normally, not a failure of adherence.

A deficit of 250 to 500 a day is the usual recommendation for sustainable loss. Larger deficits produce faster early results and worse retention of lean mass, and they get harder to sustain precisely when the metabolic adaptation is making them less effective.

## Recalculate as you go

TDEE scales with body mass. The figure that gave you a 500-calorie deficit at 90 kg is a much smaller deficit at 82 kg, which is a large part of why weight loss stalls on a fixed target.

Recalculate every four to five kilograms of change, or whenever progress flattens for more than two or three weeks. Do the same after any real change in activity — a new job, an injury, the end of a training block.

## Do not double-count exercise

If you selected an activity level that already reflects your training, do not also add the calories from a workout tracker or a smartwatch on top. That counts the same exercise twice, and it is one of the most common ways a carefully planned deficit quietly disappears.

Pick one method. Either choose the multiplier that describes your typical week and leave it alone, or select sedentary and log exercise separately against that baseline. Not both.

## What this cannot account for

Body composition, thyroid function, medication, illness, sleep, and genetics all move real expenditure, and none of them appear in the calculation. Neither does the substantial day-to-day variation in incidental movement — the difference between an active weekend and a sedentary one is easily a few hundred calories.

The number here is a hypothesis to test. Eat at it consistently for two to three weeks, track weight, and let the result correct the estimate. Your own data beats any equation on this page.
