## The payment you were quoted is not the payment you will make

Ask most people what their mortgage costs and they will tell you the principal-and-interest figure, because that is the number lenders advertise. It is also, for a lot of buyers, somewhere between 60 and 75 percent of what actually leaves the account each month.

The rest is property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance if the deposit was small, and service charges if the property is in a managed development. In the United States these are usually bundled together and collected by the lender into an escrow account, which is why the standard shorthand is PITI — principal, interest, taxes, insurance.

This calculator asks for all of it, because a budget built on the principal-and-interest number alone will be several hundred short every month.

## The formula

The monthly principal-and-interest payment on a fixed-rate amortising loan is:

```
M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ / ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)
```

where **P** is the loan amount, **r** is the monthly interest rate (the annual rate divided by 12), and **n** is the total number of monthly payments.

The structure of it is worth a moment. The payment stays constant for the whole term, but what it is doing changes completely. Interest is charged on whatever you still owe, so at the beginning — when you owe the most — almost the entire payment is interest. As the balance falls, the interest portion falls with it and more of each fixed payment goes to principal.

## Worked example

A 400,000 home with 80,000 down, so a 320,000 loan, at 6.5 percent over 30 years.

- Monthly rate: 6.5 ÷ 100 ÷ 12 = 0.0054167
- Number of payments: 30 × 12 = 360
- (1 + r)ⁿ = 1.0054167³⁶⁰ = 6.9917
- M = 320,000 × 0.0054167 × 6.9917 ÷ (6.9917 − 1)
- M = **2,022.62 per month**

Over 360 payments that comes to 728,142 — meaning **408,142 in interest**, more than the house cost.

Now the first payment. Interest for month one is 320,000 × 0.0054167 = **1,733.33**. The remaining **289.28** reduces the balance. Roughly 86 percent of your first payment buys you nothing but the right to keep borrowing.

That ratio unwinds slowly. On these terms the crossover — the first month where more goes to principal than to interest — arrives at month 233, a little over 19 years in.

## Where the rest of the payment comes from

### Property tax

Charged annually by your local authority as a percentage of assessed value, then divided into monthly instalments and held in escrow. Rates vary enormously by location — well under 0.5 percent of value in some US states and over 2 percent in others — which means two identical houses can differ by several hundred a month on tax alone.

Use your actual assessment if you have it. A national average will mislead you in either direction.

### Homeowners insurance

Required by every lender, because they are lending against the building. Priced on rebuild cost rather than purchase price, and increasingly on climate exposure — premiums in wildfire and hurricane regions have moved sharply in recent years.

### Private mortgage insurance

If your down payment is under 20 percent of the purchase price, a conventional loan will normally require PMI. It insures the lender, not you, against your default, and it is charged as a percentage of the original loan amount — typically somewhere between 0.3 and 1.5 percent annually depending on your credit score and loan-to-value ratio.

The important part is that it ends. Under the US Homeowners Protection Act your lender must cancel PMI automatically once the balance reaches 78 percent of the original value, provided payments are current, and you may request cancellation at 80 percent. On a loan with 10 percent down at 6.5 percent, that takes roughly nine years of scheduled payments — or considerably less if the property appreciates and you request a new valuation.

FHA loans work differently. On most FHA mortgages written since 2013 the mortgage insurance premium lasts the entire loan term regardless of equity, and the usual way out is refinancing to a conventional loan.

### HOA and service charges

Not collected by the lender, but they leave the same account on the same day. Condominium and managed-community fees can run to several hundred a month, and unlike your interest rate they are not fixed for 30 years.

## Term length: the real trade-off

Shortening the term raises the monthly payment and cuts the total interest, but not proportionally — and the asymmetry is larger than most people expect.

Take the same 320,000 at 6.5 percent:

| Term | Monthly P&I | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 years | 2,022.62 | 408,142 |
| 20 years | 2,385.83 | 252,600 |
| 15 years | 2,787.54 | 181,758 |

Moving from 30 years to 15 adds about 765 a month — a 38 percent increase — and removes about 226,000 in interest, a 55 percent reduction.

Whether that is the right trade depends on what the 764 would otherwise do. Prepaying a mortgage is a guaranteed return equal to your interest rate; investing the difference is a higher expected return with real variance. It also depends on flexibility: a 30-year loan with voluntary overpayments gives you most of the interest saving while letting you stop overpaying in a bad year. A 15-year loan does not.

## Overpaying

Because interest accrues on the outstanding balance, any extra payment removes that amount from every future interest calculation for the rest of the term.

On the 30-year example, adding 10 percent to the payment — about 202 a month — clears the loan in 280 months instead of 360. That is 23 years and 4 months rather than 30: just under seven years removed, for an amount most budgets can absorb.

Two practical points. Confirm your lender applies extra payments to principal rather than holding them as prepaid instalments, because those are not the same thing. And check for early repayment charges: they are uncommon in the US but standard on UK fixed-rate deals during the fixed period.

## Interest rate is not APR

The interest rate determines your payment. The APR bundles lender fees, discount points, and mortgage insurance into a single annual figure representing the cost of the loan.

This calculator uses the interest rate, because that is what the payment formula takes. When you are comparing two offers, compare the APRs — a lower headline rate bought with expensive points can easily be the worse deal.

## What this calculator cannot do

It does not know your credit file, your debt-to-income ratio, or the property type, all of which move the rate a lender will actually offer you.

It assumes a fixed rate for the full term. Adjustable-rate mortgages, and UK-style fixed periods that revert to a variable rate after two or five years, will not behave like this.

It ignores closing costs, which typically run 2 to 5 percent of the loan and are paid up front rather than monthly.

It assumes property tax and insurance stay flat. They will not — reassessments and premium increases mean an escrow payment that rises most years even on a fixed-rate loan.

Use the result to work out what you can afford and to compare scenarios against each other. Use a lender's written estimate to decide anything.
