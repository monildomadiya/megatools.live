## What BMI actually measures

Body mass index compares your weight to your height, and nothing else. It was devised in the 1830s by the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet, who was not studying obesity at all — he was looking for a way to describe the average build of a population. The measure only acquired its modern name and its modern job in 1972, when the physiologist Ancel Keys tested several weight-for-height formulas and concluded that weight divided by height squared tracked body fat better than the alternatives, at least across large groups of people.

That history explains both the strength and the weakness of the number this calculator gives you. BMI is excellent at what it was built for: comparing populations cheaply, using two measurements anyone can take with a tape and a scale. It is much weaker at describing one specific person, because a single ratio cannot tell the difference between a kilogram of muscle and a kilogram of fat, or between fat carried around the hips and fat packed around the liver.

Treat the result as a starting point. It tells you which questions are worth asking next.

## How to calculate BMI yourself

The formula is short enough to do on a phone.

**Metric.** Divide your weight in kilograms by your height in metres, squared.

```
BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²
```

**US units.** Multiply your weight in pounds by 703, then divide by your height in inches, squared. The 703 is simply the conversion factor that makes pounds-and-inches produce the same number as kilograms-and-metres.

```
BMI = 703 × weight (lb) ÷ height (in)²
```

### Worked example — metric

Someone weighing 70 kg at 1.75 m tall:

- Height squared: 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625
- BMI: 70 ÷ 3.0625 = **22.9**

That falls inside the healthy weight band.

### Worked example — US units

Someone weighing 185 lb at 5 ft 9 in (69 inches):

- Height squared: 69 × 69 = 4,761
- Weight × 703: 185 × 703 = 130,055
- BMI: 130,055 ÷ 4,761 = **27.3**

That falls in the overweight band — but see the section on limitations before drawing any conclusion from it.

Notice how sensitive the result is to height. Adding a single inch to that second example drops the BMI to 26.5. If you are unsure of your height to the nearest centimetre, your BMI is uncertain by roughly half a point, which matters when you are sitting near a boundary.

## BMI categories for adults

These are the World Health Organization's classifications for adults aged 20 and over. They are the same for men and women.

| BMI | Category |
| --- | --- |
| Below 18.5 | Underweight |
| 18.5 – 24.9 | Healthy weight |
| 25.0 – 29.9 | Overweight |
| 30.0 – 34.9 | Obesity, class I |
| 35.0 – 39.9 | Obesity, class II |
| 40.0 and above | Obesity, class III |

The obesity bands are worth splitting out rather than treating as one block. The health risks associated with a BMI of 31 and a BMI of 44 are not comparable, and clinical guidance — including which treatments are considered — often changes at 35 and again at 40.

## Your healthy weight range

Running the formula backwards is often more useful than the BMI number itself. Multiply 18.5 and 24.9 by your height in metres squared, and you get the weight range that would place you in the healthy band.

At 1.75 m, that range is 56.7 kg to 76.3 kg — a spread of nearly 20 kg. The calculator above works this out for you in whichever units you selected. Seeing the range rather than a single target tends to be more useful, because it makes clear how wide "healthy weight" actually is.

## Where BMI stops being reliable

This is the part most BMI calculators skip, and it is the part that determines whether your result means anything.

### It cannot tell muscle from fat

Muscle tissue is denser than fat. Someone who lifts seriously, plays professional rugby, or does heavy physical work can carry enough lean mass to land in the overweight or even obese range while holding very little body fat. The formula has no way to detect this. If you train with weights several times a week, your BMI is probably overstating your risk, and a body fat measurement or a simple waist measurement will tell you far more.

The reverse also happens, and gets less attention. A sedentary person with little muscle can sit comfortably inside the healthy band while carrying a high proportion of body fat — sometimes described as being "normal weight obese". A healthy BMI is not by itself evidence of a healthy body composition.

### The thresholds are not universal

A WHO expert consultation published in *The Lancet* in 2004 reviewed evidence that Asian populations develop type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at lower BMI values than European populations do. The consultation stopped short of redefining overweight and obesity globally. Instead it kept the existing cut-offs for international comparison and added a set of public health action points — 23.0, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 — that individual countries could adopt for their own populations.

Several have. If you are of South Asian, Chinese, Japanese or South-East Asian descent, a BMI of 24 may carry a risk profile closer to what 27 or 28 means in the European data the standard bands were drawn from.

### It was not designed for children

Children and adolescents are still growing, and their body composition changes substantially year on year. A BMI of 20 can be entirely healthy at 16 and high at 6. Paediatric assessment uses BMI-for-age percentile charts, comparing a child against others of the same age and sex, rather than the fixed adult cut-offs above. Do not apply this calculator's categories to anyone under 20.

### Pregnancy, older adults, and very tall or very short people

BMI is not meaningful during pregnancy. In older adults, muscle mass declines with age, so a stable BMI can conceal a real shift toward more fat and less lean tissue — and at the other end, a very low BMI in later life is associated with worse outcomes, not better. Because the formula squares height, it also tends to overstate BMI slightly in very tall people and understate it in very short people.

## The measure worth pairing with BMI

Because BMI cannot see *where* fat sits, guidance has increasingly moved toward measuring waist as well. Fat stored around the abdomen — visceral fat — carries more metabolic risk than the same amount stored on the hips and thighs, and BMI treats the two identically.

NICE guideline NG246 now recommends that adults with a BMI below 35 measure their waist-to-height ratio alongside BMI. The calculation is simple: waist circumference divided by height, both in the same units.

| Waist-to-height ratio | Central adiposity |
| --- | --- |
| 0.40 – 0.49 | Healthy |
| 0.50 – 0.59 | Increased |
| 0.60 and above | High |

The rule of thumb that falls out of this is easy to remember: **keep your waist to less than half your height**. Measure at the midpoint between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone, at the end of a normal breath out, with the tape snug but not compressing the skin.

Above a BMI of 35, the waist measurement adds little — at that point BMI is already a strong enough signal on its own.

## What to do with the number

If your BMI sits comfortably inside the healthy band and your waist is under half your height, there is not much more to extract from these two measurements.

If either one sits near a boundary, the useful next step is not to recalculate more precisely. It is to look at the things BMI cannot see: waist measurement over time, blood pressure, blood glucose, and how your weight has trended over the past few years. A BMI of 26 that has been stable for a decade is a different picture from a BMI of 26 that was 22 two years ago.

And if the number surprises you — in either direction — that is usually a sign that BMI is doing what it does badly, and that a conversation with a clinician will be worth more than another calculation.
